Ask a good creative team how they know a brief is right and you get a look, not a sentence. The knowledge is real and it is nowhere. It lives in who reviews what, in the examples people reach for, in the three things a senior person always catches. Every new hire learns it the slow way, and every model starts from zero.

Encoding is the act of writing that layer down in a form that can be reused. Not a manual, which nobody reads, and not a prompt, which nobody maintains. A layer: the rules a team actually applies, the examples it holds up as the standard, the sources it trusts, and the loops it runs when something is off.

## Four things worth encoding

Rules are the constraints that never move. Tone, format, what the brand refuses. They are short and they are boring, and they are the first thing a model gets wrong without them.

Examples are the standard made visible. Three good ones beat a page of description, because a model, like a new colleague, learns the shape from the specimen.

Sources are where the facts come from. A product sheet, a research base, a set of past briefs. Naming them is what stops the model inventing.

Loops are what happens after the first draft. Who checks, against what, and what a revision is allowed to change. This is the part most teams forget, and it is where the quality lives.

## Why it is the team's, not the vendor's

A vendor can sell you a tool that runs. It cannot sell you the layer, because the layer is your judgment. Once it is encoded, it works from any model, it survives a model change, and it belongs to the people who wrote it. That is the difference between renting a capability and owning one.
