## ONE · THE REALITY (≈6 min)

### 01 · The reality (5:00)

Within two years, digital content and digital products are a commodity.

The cost of making one more image, one more video, one more app, drops to almost nothing. In 2022 I
made one picture in Midjourney. This year our AI films ran as paid media and Claude built whole tools
for the teams. That's three years. Think about what the next two do.

### 02 · Two labs read the whole internet (6:00)

Why so fast? Two reasons I want to be honest about.

First, the models were trained on everything. Every picture, every codebase, every book that was
online, a big part of human culture, and nobody was really paid for it. Anthropic and OpenAI broke a
social contract there. I think that's unfair, and I also think it's not coming back.

Second, what they built with it. What a software company puts on a two-year roadmap, these labs ship
as a feature of the chat window. In weeks.

And it doesn't stay with them. Give it months, and a model on your own laptop does what the frontier
does today. Regulation can slow that down, a lab can hold a release back. It won't stop intelligence
becoming a utility, something you pay for by the meter, like electricity.

### 03 · Rebuilt (8:00)

*Switch to the browser: the open-source clone.*

So here's the obvious fear. One person rebuilt an Adobe app and Word, open source, with AI. [Name
the project and the person.]

Should Adobe be worried? Look closely and it's not as good. And no company leaves a subscription for
a clone, because bringing new software into a team is already painful enough. So this is not where
the threat sits.

### 04 · The threat (9:00)

The threat is that nobody opens the other software.

People make the image, the film, the deck inside Claude or ChatGPT. They convert it to whatever format
they need. And when something doesn't work, they say "this doesn't work" and it gets fixed. That's the
whole interface.

*Switch to the browser: one of your artifacts.*

This is a Claude artifact. I could have shown my whole website like this tonight, without hosting
anything. People are still making PowerPoints, but they're also making these.

So every digital tool gets absorbed into those two windows. Right now that's Claude and ChatGPT,
because they're the only ones good enough to build software like this on the fly. Give it a year and
an open model will do it too. For a lot of our industry that's the end of something.

## TWO · AN INTELLIGENCE (≈5 min)

### 05 · Not software (11:00)

If it's a commodity like electricity, why does it feel so different?

Because electricity doesn't solve anything. Build more power plants and you run more machines, but
nothing gets solved. Turn all the salt water in the world into drinking water, and you have more
water. Add intelligence, and more problems get solved. That's the curve no other commodity has.

For anyone frowning right now: this is not about consciousness, or feelings, or whether it's like us.
I don't need that debate tonight. These models were trained on our language and they reason through
problems. That's what intelligence is: the ability to solve complex problems.

### 06 · No ceiling yet (12:30)

*The curve. Point at the axes, not the model names.*

On the vertical axis: what it can finish. On the horizontal: more intelligence. Every release makes
fewer small mistakes, so it gets further on long, difficult work.

Anyone who has worked with Opus for a while knows this feeling. You throw a harder problem at it and
it just goes further. No upper bound has shown up yet.

### 07 · An intelligence (13:30)

And that's why it's so hard to design for. Sometimes you tell it exactly what to do, like a tool.
Sometimes you explain what you're after and let it figure out the steps, like a collaborator. Nothing
we designed for before was both.

So it's not software, it's an intelligence. That's the title. That's the one sentence I'd like you to
take home.

*Optional, one line, only if the room is with you:* There's a version of this where it takes all the
work, but that's bad economics, and people won't accept it. That's a different talk.

## THREE · WHAT HAPPENED WHEN I TRUSTED IT (≈5 min)

### 08 · Tom on the Moon (16:00)

So what does it look like when you treat it as an intelligence instead of a tool?

Tom on the Moon, with Christof. A brand world for an agency of creators. I didn't sit there prompting
picture by picture. I gave Claude the brief, the references, a few API keys and a way to check its
own frames. And then I let it run.

Wide first: four ways to read the brief. Ten worlds, the same walk in each. Then narrow: one planet,
the Mineral Dunes and the Living Arches. And then one frame the client called perfect.

### 09 · The checks (17:30)

*Press run on the bench.*

The trick is here. The brief became rules a model reads, and checks it runs on its own frames before
anyone looks. Watch: the anchor passes. A vista goes back with a note. And our own Moon is refused,
because it's the wrong moon.

That's the part designers are good at, by the way. Knowing what good looks like, and saying why.

### 10 · One wave, on the wall (18:30)

Once the world is written down, the frames come in waves. Drawn, graded and delivered while nobody's
watching. You stop judging a picture and start judging a wall.

### 11 · Nine sectors, one world (19:15)

Same approach for In The Pocket: one key visual per sector, from finance to public transport. Two
references anchored every draw, so nine briefs came back as one place.

Clients were happy. And honestly, so was I, because I spent my time on the judging and not on the
making.

## FOUR · TWO WAYS OUT (≈2 min)

### 12 · When making costs nothing (20:00)

So if making costs nothing, where does the value go? I think to the two ends.

One end is the authentic. Cinema is doing well. Festivals sell out. Hedge funds are buying festivals,
and at Loop we chase festival deals for a reason. The AI slop is the end of a social media that was
dying anyway, and maybe it had to happen to show what AI content really looks like. Don't compete
with AI on that axis: the harder it tries to look human, the less human it reads. It's the uncanny
valley, all the way down.

The other end is the intelligence itself. And that's where this room comes in.

## FIVE · WHAT IS LEFT TO DESIGN (≈4 min)

### 13 · The turn (22:00)

So what's left to design? The interface is the intelligence.

People will build what they need on the fly, and fix it by saying what's wrong. The way we built
software, architecture, frontend, backend, a feature roadmap, stops being the scarce part. Yes,
security matters, but most of this will live inside enterprises anyway.

And yes, a page Claude designs is salmon with a serif. That's a default. Defaults get changed.

### 14 · Interfaces for and with an intelligence (23:00)

Four things I'd work on if I were you.

What it knows about your work: the models are smart enough, they lack your context, so somebody
writes down what the team knows.

What good looks like, as checks: taste moves, so the people who have it keep rewriting the checks.

A shared language between minds: our agents will read what theirs want, and the other way round. New
economies will run on that, and somebody has to design how those two meet.

And interfaces nobody has drawn yet: built, shown and changed on the fly, for one person and one
moment. Most of them will never be a product.

### 15 · Headless or told (24:30)

One more distinction, because not all of it is equal.

Where your story matters, tell it yourself, and encode it deeper than a website. Shader libraries. A
design system shipped as source, the way shadcn does it, so every intelligence that renders you
renders you right. Motion and type rules a model can read. Checks for what's on brand.

Where it doesn't, a financial dashboard, internal tools, reporting: go headless. Ship the data and the
rules, and let each person's intelligence draw the view they need.

The range a brand can express itself in has only gotten wider. It just asks for a different way of
thinking.
