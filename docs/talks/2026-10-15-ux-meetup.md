## THE TALK (≈16 min)

### 01 · The reality (4:00)

*Let it sit for a beat before you speak.*

Within two years, digital content and digital products are a commodity.

The cost of making one more image, one more video, one more app drops to almost nothing. And there
are two reasons it goes this fast, which I want to be honest about.

First, the models were trained on everything. Every picture, every codebase, every book online, a
big part of human culture, and nobody was really paid for it. Anthropic and OpenAI broke a social
contract there. I think that's unfair, and I also think it's not coming back.

Second, what they build with it. What a software company puts on a two-year roadmap, these labs ship
as a feature of the chat window, in weeks. And it doesn't stay with them: give it months and a model
on your own laptop does what the frontier does today. Regulation can slow it down. It won't stop it
becoming a utility, something you pay for by the meter, like electricity.

### 02 · Photoshop, rebuilt with Claude (6:30)

Here's what that looks like. This is PhotoCraft. One developer, Brandon Thomas, rebuilt Photoshop in
Rust with Claude and put it on GitHub on 30 September. Over sixty thousand stars in two weeks. A week
later: Word, Excel and PowerPoint. *(The name is from the press coverage; the repo itself credits the
ArtCraft project.)*

So, should Adobe be worried? Look closely and it's early alpha. And no company leaves a subscription
for a clone, because bringing new software into a team is painful enough already. So that's not
where the threat sits.

### 03 · The threat (8:00)

The threat is that nobody opens the other software.

People make the image, the film, the deck inside Claude or ChatGPT. They convert it to whatever format
they need. And when something doesn't work they say "this doesn't work", and it gets fixed. That's
the whole interface.

*Switch to the browser: one of your artifacts.*

This is a Claude artifact. I could have shown you my whole website like this tonight, without hosting
anything. People still make PowerPoints, but they're also making these.

So every digital tool gets absorbed into those two windows. For a lot of our industry that's the end
of something.

### 04 · A commodity like electricity, except this one solves problems (10:00)

But if it's a commodity like electricity, why does it feel so different?

Because electricity doesn't solve anything. Build more power plants and you run more machines.
Turn all the salt water in the world into drinking water and you have more water. Add intelligence
and more problems get solved.

*Point at the axes, not the model names.*

Up: what it can finish. Across: more intelligence. Every release makes fewer small mistakes, so it
gets further on long, difficult work. Anyone who's worked with Opus for a while knows the feeling:
you throw a harder problem at it and it just goes further. No upper bound has shown up yet.

And for anyone frowning: this isn't about consciousness or feelings. I don't need that debate
tonight. These models were trained on our language and they reason through problems. That's what
intelligence is.

### 05 · Two ways out (12:30)

So when making costs nothing, where does the value go? I think to two ends.

One end is what only people make. Cinema is doing well. Festivals sell out. Hedge funds are buying
festivals, and at Loop we chase festival deals for a reason. The AI slop is the end of a social media
that was dying anyway. Don't compete with AI on that axis: the harder it tries to look human, the
less human it reads.

The other end is the intelligence itself. And that's where this room comes in.

### 06 · The interface is the intelligence (14:00)

*Let them read the stack before you talk.*

People will build what they need on the fly and fix it by saying what's wrong. The way we built
software, architecture, frontend, backend, a feature roadmap, stops being the scarce part.

So look at it as a stack. At the bottom, the models: a commodity. On them, the intelligence, Claude
and ChatGPT. On top, the interfaces people build for themselves: a dashboard, a report, an internal
tool, a page for one person. Where your story doesn't matter, go headless. Ship the data and the rules
and let them build.

But where your story matters, tell it yourself. That's the gold layer. Your brand, encoded so every
intelligence that renders you renders you right: shader libraries, a design system shipped as
source the way shadcn does it, motion and type rules a model can read, checks for what's on brand.

And yes, a page Claude designs comes out salmon with a serif. That's a default. Defaults get
changed. The range a brand can express itself in has only gotten wider; it just asks for a different
way of thinking.

### 07 · The most interesting time to be a designer (17:00)

So yes, the downstream part is solved, or will be soon. But interfaces and experiences nobody has
conceived yet are now ours to make. I think this is the most interesting time to be a designer.

## THE ENDING · the thesis and the Arc (≈5 min)

### The thesis (18:00)

*The corridor opens: "AI sits somewhere between tool and collaborator."*

Remember the title. It's not software, it's an intelligence. Sometimes you tell it exactly what to
do, like a tool. Sometimes you explain what you're after and let it work it out, like a
collaborator. Nothing we designed for before was both.

So the real question isn't which model. It's where and how this new kind of intelligence should
take part in the work.

### Navigate · Encode · Build (19:00)

*Scroll through the three stations; one line each.*

That's how I work now. Navigate: learn how this intelligence behaves before you hand it work.
Encode: write down what your team knows and what good looks like, because the models are smart
enough, they just lack your context. Build: give each piece of work its own setup, built on what
the team wrote down, with people steering.
