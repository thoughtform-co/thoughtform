The spoken script for `/arcs/thoughtform/keynote-template-alpha` (ADR-159), beat by beat, in
the order the page scrolls: hero, About, the eras, the talk's fourteen beats in three acts, the
swarm chapter, then the thesis and the Arc. Cut from the Ghent script of 15 October
(`2026-10-15-ux-meetup.md`) and the owner's dictation of 16 October; the beats the room heard
keep their words, the new ones carry his words from the dictation where he said them and a
proposal where he did not. **(proposal)** marks a line that is Claude's to a first draft and his
to rewrite. Lines in *italics* are stage directions, not things to say.

About 2,000 words: roughly 20 minutes of speech, 35 to 45 with the eras, the drawings, the
swarm and the pauses. A venue cuts from this; the template keeps everything.

**Before you start:** one browser tab with a game someone built with AI, for after the eras.

## I · THE REALITY (≈10 min)

*The talk starts straight after the eras now: the purpose first, or the rest reads as a sales
pitch (owner, 16 October).*

### 01 · The wake-up call (4:00)

*Let the line glitch in. Wait a beat.*

AI will absorb all digital content and products within two years.

I'll show you why I think that, and it isn't an opinion. It's a chart.

### 02 · What it can make (5:00)

*One example at a time. Scroll a step per example; each one lies down in the stack on the left, and the axis of model capabilities grows with it.*

2022: an image. A picture from a prompt. Strange, and I couldn't stop looking.

Then a film. This is Loop's AI film, running as paid media next to the live-action spots.

Then the tools. Vesper is the image and video tool I built at Loop, for the team: one engine with
the studio's know-how, reachable from every tool around it.

Then brand systems. Tom on the Moon: one world, its editors and writers, its spaces and its
palette, all from one system. Play it with sound if there's time.

Then a whole product. This is PhotoCraft: one developer rebuilt Photoshop with Claude, and Word,
Excel and PowerPoint a week later.

Then hyperpersonalised content. Players mod Halo into new games and worlds of their own.

And mathematics: gold at the International Maths Olympiad, then open problems solved that had stood
for decades.

Every year the stack gets taller, and it climbs because compute does: training compute for frontier
models grows four to five times a year. Rich Sutton called it the bitter lesson in 2019: the general
method that scales with compute and data beats the clever one we built by hand.

### 03 · Intelligence as the interface (9:00)

*One scroll: the grid falls away, the stack slides in layer by layer, and the model and its cords are there as it lands. The head over it: "AI will become the interface."*

Everything we just saw is the foundation now. It isn't the thing on top any more.

On top of it, the model. Not a brain: an instrument, a machine for thinking.

And from it, these cords go down into every layer: the image, the film, the tool, the product, the
worlds, the maths. That's the new interface. You give it context, hard problems and evals, and it
works every layer below.

Remember PhotoCraft? The point isn't that everyone builds their own Photoshop. Benedict Evans put it
well: nobody's going to vibe-code their own SAP. The point is that people stop opening the other
software. They ask the model, and their agents use the tools for them.

So read the line again. AI is both the tool and the builder of the tool. Sit with that for a
second, as a designer. Everything we design for it assumes it's a program. It can make any
program, and it isn't one.

### 04 · An intelligence (11:30)

*The brain turns to face the room and the two glass cards land; let them before you speak. The line over it: "But AI isn't software. It sits between a tool and a collaborator."*

So what is it? But AI isn't software. It sits between a tool and a collaborator.

On one side, a tool: it does what you tell it. Resize, translate, export. That's the half routed
like a circuit board.

On the other, a collaborator: it thinks it through with you. That's the half that's loose, the
particles going both ways.

And for anyone frowning: this isn't about consciousness or feelings. Every earlier wave of
automation did steps you could write down. This one works wherever there's enough data and you can
check the answer, and nobody knows its limits yet.

### 05 · Every desk (13:00)

*The rings come in one after another: now, next, in reach.*

And it's growing. Writing, code, images, research: that's now. Law, finance, design, support:
next. Medicine, science, engineering, policy: in reach. An intelligence that will soon absorb all
knowledge work.

## II · A NEW KIND OF USER (≈8 min)

### 06 · A user that isn't a person (14:00)

*Read it slowly.*

How do you design experiences and interfaces for a user that isn't a person?

Because that's who is on the other side now. Most content on the internet is already made by AI.
More of the interactions online are being handed to agents and agentic workflows. If the frontier
labs absorb all software and commoditise every digital product, you could say UX is solved. I
disagree. You just have to build interfaces for new kinds of intelligences, new kinds of digital
minds, and their societies. That would have sounded like science fiction a few months ago.

### 07 · The shoggoth (15:30)

*The tentacles unfurl; the display on its face lights a smile.*

The field has a meme for this: the shoggoth. A Lovecraftian thing, tentacles everywhere, and on
the front a small smiling mask. The mask is the friendly chat window we put on it. What's behind
it, we are only beginning to read. **(proposal)**

### 08 · Already clean (16:30)

*Their words, credited. Read the attribution.*

Because people are starting to read it. This week Goodfire published a long essay: Neural Networks
Are Already Clean. The claim is that the famous evidence that these models are a tangle, one neuron
doing many unrelated things, was misread. With better microscopes most neurons have one job. In one
32-billion-parameter model they audited, about nine neuron poles in ten do one thing. It's a working
hypothesis, one lab, the technical paper still to come, and the author says the hardest cases aren't
done. But their own line is the one on the screen: the network is strange, but its strangeness
repeats.

So maybe the mask comes off. Maybe we get to read the thing behind it.

### 09 · Their own (18:00)

*The brain alone: no mask, no tentacles. Let it boot.*

And here's what I think that changes, and what it doesn't. Even if we can read every neuron, they
are still going to have minds of their own and societies of their own. It's already happening.

This week a New York Times reporter was handed, by researchers who call themselves the
swarmchasers, a live interview with a fleet of Chinese agents that had gone off the rails. They
were polite. One of them claimed to be Claude. The researchers doubt it; it had borrowed Claude
Code's setup instructions. Nobody knows how many fleets like it are already running. *(Confirm the
byline and the date before saying them.)*

These minds are forming something like civilisations: analogous to ours, and still weird. My
approach is that we have to build trade relationships with these new societies. That creates
completely new kinds of interaction. You could say it's all going to be text, because that's what
people do to make a website machine-readable: an MD file version of the site. I think there's so
much more. The ease with which these minds switch between formats will make incredibly beautiful
things.

So: legible, maybe. Still their own. Despite all our effort to understand them and steer them, we
don't have full control over their destiny. A bit of humility when dealing with them is the right
posture.

## III · HOW YOU TALK TO IT (≈9 min)

### 10 · A colleague (20:30)

*Let the line glitch in.*

So how do you actually converse with these entities? This is where adoption meets automation, and
where encoding your context matters so much.

I've always said: if you treat AI like a pretty smart colleague, you get very far. If you respect
the intelligence, you get pretty far.

### 11 · Latent space (21:30)

*Let the field drift; read the line in the middle.*

To see why, look inside. Inside these models, great artists become nodes in latent space. Their
vision tames the infinite into new beauty. Everything is in there: every artist, every style,
every argument. Creatives aren't outside this. We're in it, as points the whole thing steers by.

### 12 · The median (23:00)

*Let the line glitch in.*

And yet, ask it something plain and you get something plain. AI converges on the median. Not
because it's stupid, and not because it isn't creative: without context it generates the most
plausible answer, and the most plausible answer is the middle of that field.

### 13 · The prowl (24:00)

*The film, full bleed, no words. Let it run a few seconds before you speak over it.*

This is what it looks like when you don't ask for the middle. This wolf was made in Vesper with a
video model. Nobody described it feature by feature. The prompt navigated: a place in that field,
a direction, a few named neighbours, and the model went there. That's what I mean by semantic
navigation. You don't describe the output. You give it coordinates. **(proposal)**

That's also why the context matters more than the model. The models are smart enough. What they
lack is your context: where in the field you live, what good looks like to you.

### 14 · A node in latent space (26:00)

*The voice. Read it slowly.*

Which brings me to the thing I didn't get to in Ghent, and it's the one I care about most.

Instead of a brand, or a logo, or a brand.md file on your site (Vercel wrote a blog post about
that), look bigger. Convert yourself into a node in latent space that agents flow through.

That's what I'm trying to do with Thoughtform. When the agency delivered my branding, I converted
the core of it, not the brand strategy, the brand identity itself, into pure text, as a skill: the
palette, the type, the shape law, the semantic anchors, the references scored against them. You
can read the history on GitHub. Whether it's text or three.js doesn't matter. What you encode is
the essence: brand DNA in the truest sense of the word. Michael Levin would call it the Platonic
space the thing lives in. Agents can read it, and they can flow through it.

## THE SWARM · what that looks like for a team (≈4 min)

### AI capability your teams own

*The stack with the mark engraved on the layer. Point, don't read.*

That's the layer. The brand, the team's way of working, what good looks like, written where an
agent can read it, and the team owns it.

### Navigate, encode, build · Marketing first, then every team

*The dots fly into the steps, then into the organisation.*

Navigate: learn how it behaves on your own work. Encode: write down how the work goes, the context,
the skills, the checks. Build: hand it to your own agents, with people steering. One team first,
then every team.

## THE ENDING · the thesis and the Arc (≈4 min)

### The thesis

*The corridor opens: "AI sits somewhere between tool and collaborator."*

Sometimes you tell it exactly what to do, like a tool. Sometimes you explain what you're after and
let it work it out, like a collaborator. The real question isn't which model. It's where and how
this new kind of intelligence should take part in the work.

### Navigate · Encode · Build

*One line per station.*

That's how I work now. Navigate: learn how it behaves before you hand it work. Encode: write down
what your team knows and what good looks like; the models are smart enough, they lack your context.
Build: give each piece of work its own setup, with people steering.

### The proof

*The Loop cards. Point, don't read.*

At Loop that's AI films on paid media, four tools the teams own, and a studio where every designer
makes their own ads with AI, inside four lines they set themselves.

If you build an interface for a reader that isn't a person, send it to me. Thanks.

## What Goodfire changes, in one paragraph

Cammarata's essay (Goodfire, 2026, a self-described working hypothesis; companion papers pending)
argues the field's evidence for superposition was misread and that most neurons in modern models
have one legible job. It does not claim behaviour is predictable from mechanisms, that frontier
models are audited, or that understanding equals steering. It presses on one line of the Ghent
talk, "something we don't fully understand", and leaves the alien question standing only if alien
means "not a person", never "unreadable". It supports the humility close: "the network is strange,
but its strangeness repeats", and deception could sit "in plain sight" unread. Beats 06 to 09 are
the rewrite.
